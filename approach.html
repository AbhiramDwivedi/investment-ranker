Cheap against itself

One rule decides what gets looked at. This is the rule, the evidence for and against it, and the things it cannot see.

A stock is interesting when it is cheap against its own ten-year valuation history — not down from its high, not cheap against its peers. The arithmetic runs every weekday night on every S&P 1500 name with an SEC filing history, with no language model anywhere in the loop. The one question arithmetic cannot answer — is the cheapness explained? — is the only thing a written memo is for, and the only thing a person decides.

Where to see it. The Ranker's first table, "Tonight's cut," is this rule's output — every name in the cheapest 30% of its own history on at least two bases, with the quality check beside it and the blocked names still shown. The pattern table below it answers a different question: what is already moving.

The premise has been tested against a decade of the index, and the honest summary is on the unflattering side: alone, it has no edge. Two valuation bases agreeing, and a market that is itself on sale, do carry information. The evidence section below shows the numbers that say so.

What "cheap against itself" means

For every name the engine rebuilds ten years of daily P/E, P/S and P/FCF — P/E and P/B for financials — point-in-time: each day's multiple uses the trailing-twelve-month figure as it was known that day, on the filing date, against the split-adjusted close. Today's multiple is then ranked inside that history. The 2nd percentile means the stock is cheaper than it was 98% of the past decade.

Three legs are read together, never one: the median of three when at least two agree, the higher of two when both agree, and a single leg is flagged as thin. One line can lie. Three rarely lie the same way — and the backtest below is the reason that rule exists.

Five checks stop the percentile from lying. Trailing GAAP earnings are tested against consensus before they are trusted, so one-time gains cannot manufacture a cheap P/E. Cheapness is labelled de-rated (the price fell) or earned (earnings jumped while the price held) — both can be good buys, but only the first is on sale. A fair band is built from the stock's own 20th-percentile multiple, with a caveat when that floor is itself rich, because a growth-era floor is not a floor. A reverse-DCF asks what ten-year earnings growth the current price needs. And a replay asks whether buying this name at its own lows has ever actually paid.

How a night runs

The numbers in this note are from the mid-August 2026 run; the Ranker's step-by-step panel shows tonight's.

  1. Data. fetch_facts.py · fetch_estimates.py
    SEC EDGAR company facts for every S&P 1500 name — 10 to 15 years of quarterly earnings, revenue, cash flow, share count, debt and cash, each point stamped with its filing date — cached and refreshed on a rolling budget. Prices from Yahoo. Free, and about 15 minutes.
  2. Signal engine. signals.evaluate() · universe_own_history.py
    One function per name: valuation, quality gate, economics, fundamental momentum, trend and events, sentiment, market window. Filings only for the roughly 1,420 index names, with analyst estimates layered on for the smaller covered set. No network, and the thresholds live in the code, changing only by a dated commit.
  3. Scores and flags.
    A 0-100 order of reading, a candidate rule that means "worth a memo," a tier that says act or wait, a three-family sell test, and short-horizon flags that are shown but never sized like a position.
  4. The decision box. advisor.py
    Deterministic. It annotates each candidate, says whether the machine agrees with every entry price a person has already set, and lists the next action. It downgrades; it never upgrades.
  5. The memo.
    One per night, two pages, judgment only. It pastes the engine's numbers verbatim, sources what changed from filings, and answers whether the impairment is real. It never recomputes, never projects and never discounts.
  6. The analyst.
    Reads the memo and decides whether to believe it. Only a person sets an entry price. Automation never upgrades, never arms and never trades.

What the engine measures

Seven families per name. Valuation is the heart — and the evidence cut its weight in half.

FamilyPointsWhat it asks
Valuation20 × agreementWhere the multiple sits in its own ten-year distribution, scaled 1.0 / 0.75 / 0.25 for three, two or one agreeing bases. It carried 40 points until the backtest showed the level of cheapness carried no information and agreement did.
Quality gate25Pass, watch or fail. Hard fails: burning cash at a loss, net debt over 4× EBITDA, share count up 25% in three years. Soft flags cover weak cash conversion, margin erosion and stock compensation. Unknowns are listed, never counted.
EconomicscontextReturn on invested capital, and the growth rate the business can fund without outside capital. When the price needs more growth than the business can fund, that is a penalty.
Fundamental momentum25Revenue growth and whether it accelerated, forward-earnings revisions over 30 and 90 days, analysts raising minus cutting. Names with no estimates read unknown, never zero.
Trend and events10Drawdown, distance to the 50- and 200-day averages, relative strength against the index and the sector, next earnings date, new 13D filings.
SentimentcontextShort interest, insider open-market buying, target upside. Shown, never scored.
Market window15Open when the index closes below the average of its last 200 closes. The same 15 points for every name at once — the regime, not the stock.

The candidate rule

A candidate means "worth a memo." It is never a buy.

  • cheap_pct ≤ 30Blended own-history percentile, ten years.
  • bases ≥ 2Two of the three legs agree. One leg alone was a trap in the data.
  • gate ≠ failNo hard fail, and fewer than three soft flags.
  • rev_90d ≥ −10%The street has not cut next year's earnings by a tenth. Unknown never blocks — the record says the check did not run.
  • ¬ cascadeNot a pile-on of analyst cuts. A one-year buyer loses nothing by waiting for the cuts to stop.

Blocked names are shown with the reason, never hidden. The score orders the memo queue; it decides nothing on its own.

The premise, tested

Two backtests on the S&P 500 as it actually stood at the time, 2016-2026, both pre-registered: the rules were written down before the results were seen, and changed only afterwards, only by a person, and only going forward.

 Mind the bar: the median member lost 16 points to the index over three years. A cap-weighted index led by a handful of names is hard to beat with anything that is not those names. The control absorbs that — and the rule still adds nothing to it.

The rule did what a random member did, at every horizon — and the expensive-zone mirror did the same. The level of own-history cheapness was not information about forward relative return in this decade.

What did carry information: two bases agreeing, worth 12 points at three years; the market being below its 200-day average at entry, the only group positive at 12 months and with half the risk of a 30% drawdown; and falling knives, the state the engine used to block, which turned out to be the best entry state while waiting for the bounce was the worst.

What did not: de-rated versus earned cheapness made no difference, negative cash flow at entry was no worse, and 2021-23 entries were the worst of the decade — the regime the index is arguably still in.

Track D — the buy window

Six window definitions and a mechanical decision rule, all written down before running: a window ships if enough entries fall inside it across three or more years with a wide enough gap, and the incumbent is kept unless a challenger beats it by two points on a leave-one-year-out minimum.

Five of the six cleared the bar. The best challenger — the index below its 200-day and cheap breadth above 25% — beat the incumbent by 1.3 points, under the 2-point bar, so the rule returned keep the incumbent. The window is the index below its 200-day average, unchanged. The code applied that verdict, not the reader.

Mostly selection, not timing. The control moves a point or two with the window; the entries move six to nine. Buy the index inside the window, yes. Buy the median member, no. Buy a two-basis cheap name, better than outside — and that third trade is the only one this engine makes.

One week is thick. 65 of the 840 entries land on a single date, the first row of the grid, where a name already inside the zone when its history first qualifies gets counted as an entry. Drop them — a check specified after the result, and labelled as such — and the in-window medians roughly halve and every leave-one-year-out minimum turns negative, though the in-versus-out gap survives and the rule still says keep the incumbent. Read the pre-registered table as the record and that check as the honest magnitude.

Track D is in-sample by construction and perhaps four or five real episodes wearing 840 entries' clothing. It gives the buy window no new historical claim; the window survived only because no challenger cleared a deliberately high bar. Its forward record starts Aug. 16, 2026.

The whole index, and where the estimates are

The engine used to screen 68 names that had already been chosen — a filter on what was already in view. Now one function scores every S&P 1500 name with a filing history from filings and prices alone, and the smaller covered set is simply where analyst estimates, sentiment and event data get layered on. Same weights, same four gate words, whether a name is one of 68 or one of 1,423.

Everything estimate-shaped for an uncovered name reads unknown, not zero in disguise. It adds nothing to the score, and it cannot block candidacy either: a rule that treated "we did not look" as "it failed" would exclude every name outside coverage. A filings-only score can reach 6 of the 25 momentum points, not 25 — the honest direction for a thinner record. Do not compare a filings-only score with a covered one as though they were measured the same way.

Each night the top candidates outside coverage are written to a probation list, so the next run fetches their data and the full engine can reach them. Probation moves data, never a label.

The memo

The stock is at the Nth percentile of its own ten-year valuation. Is that because the business is impaired — the de-rating deserved, the cheap multiple the new normal — or because it is out of favour, the franchise intact and the multiple due to mean-revert? That is the only question the memo exists to answer.

Two pages: what changed, dated and sourced from filings and transcripts; whether the impairment is cyclical or structural, item by item; a verdict; and three to five kill criteria — metric, threshold, and the report it gets checked against. Entry, if any, is chosen from the machine's own band, never invented. No unsourced numbers.

What it cannot see

  • GAAP noise. Trailing earnings are GAAP; write-offs and mark-to-market swings distort P/E. The blend and the flags mitigate this. They do not cure it.
  • Foreign filers have no EDGAR series and get no own-history percentile at all. That costs the index scan about 156 names, and every breadth number on the Ranker is biased a little by their absence.
  • Own-history on a de-rated growth stock is honest but incomplete. The 10th percentile of a 37× median is a fact; whether 37× was ever sustainable is the memo's question.
  • Cyclicals on trough earnings look dear on P/E and cheap on P/S. The blend handles some of it; the memo handles the rest.
  • The per-name replay is survivors, one rate regime and a handful of episodes. Use it as the question "what is different this time?" — never as a probability.
  • The premise is backtested, and alone it failed. The rules that failure motivated carry no historical claim and are evaluated forward from Aug. 16, 2026.
  • The stress leg of the window is untested — the replay cache holds no point-in-time volatility or credit data — and is the next thing to pre-register.

What never happens

  • No trades, ever. The tool proposes; a person executes.
  • No upgrade by automation. Runs may downgrade or raise an alert. Anything toward a buy needs a human-reviewed memo on file.
  • No "buy" label without that memo. A candidate is a memo-queue suggestion.
  • The decision layer is deterministic. A language model narrates it and may disagree in the log. It never overrides it.
  • "No buys today" is the expected output most days. Activity is not manufactured.
  • Scoring changes on a human decision only — pre-registered before the results are seen, forward-only, no historical claim. The model never adjusts a rule in the same session that reads the numbers.
  • Every market number carries a source date. Stale data publishes with a banner.
  • This page and the Ranker carry zero personal data. No holdings, no positions, no amounts.
