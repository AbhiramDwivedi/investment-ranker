How the ranking works

Three pictures for the whole thing: what happens on one night, what a stock is measured cheap against, and what decides the order it comes out in.

This page is the short version. The method note has the same thing in detail, with the evidence for and against it.

Picture one: a single night

Every weeknight the engine reads the same list of companies and looks for one thing. It wants a company whose price is unusually low measured against its own past. Not against its rivals, and not against its own high.

What comes out is a candidate, and a candidate means only that the name is worth a written memo. It is not a buy, and nothing on the public page ever is.

Picture two: cheap against itself

A yardstick is one way of pricing a company against its own results. The engine uses three: the price against earnings, against sales, and against the cash the business actually generates. Banks and insurers get price against book value in place of cash flow.

For each yardstick it rebuilds ten years of daily readings for that one company, then asks where today sits inside that ten-year spread. The 12th percentile means the stock was more expensive than it is now on 88% of the days of the past decade.

A single yardstick can lie. Earnings are flattered by a one-time gain, and a sales multiple looks cheap at a company that is burning through its cash. So the engine never trusts one reading. With all three available it takes the middle one, which forces two of the three to agree before the blend can read cheap. With only two available it takes the higher, less flattering one. A name left with a single yardstick is marked thin.

In the picture the dear cash-flow reading cannot spoil the verdict, and one lucky cheap reading could not have carried it alone. That is the whole point of blending.

Picture three: what sets the order

The candidates are then sorted by a buy score from zero to 100. The score is an order of reading, not a forecast of return. It answers which name a person should look at first, and nothing else.

The surprising part of that picture is how small cheapness is. It is 20 points of the 100, and it is scaled back when the yardsticks disagree: three quarters of it when two agree, a quarter when one stands alone. It used to be worth 40. The backtest described further down found that the depth of the discount said nothing about what a stock did next, while the number of agreeing yardsticks did, so the weight was halved and tied to agreement.

The 15 points for the market window are identical for every name on the same night, because the window is a fact about the market and not about any one company. It is open when the market trades below the average of its last 200 closes. On a night when it is shut, every score on the page sits 15 points lower than it could, which is why a score in the fifties can lead the list.

The last five points are the company's own record. Has buying this particular name at its own ten-year lows paid off before? Three or more past episodes that mostly beat the market add five points, and three or more that mostly lost subtract five.

Two smaller penalties can apply on top. A company loses five points when its cheapness came from earnings jumping rather than from the price falling, because that is not a discount. It loses five more when the growth its price already assumes is more than the business can fund out of its own cash.

That is the ranking. The method note carries the parts a picture cannot hold: what stops each number from lying, what the backtests found when the rule was tested on its own, and what the screen cannot see. It is the argument; this page is the mechanism.

This is a personal research tool, not licensed financial advice, and nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell any security. The numbers in the pictures are real output from a single run, so a later run will differ.
