Two tables, three questions. The first asks whether a stock is cheap against its own history and whether its filings hold up; the second asks whether it is already moving. Neither is a forecast, and neither knows anything about competition — that is what the memos are for. These are pattern scores, not predictions. The second list ranks how strongly each stock matches patterns that came before big gains in the past. It does not and cannot say who doubles next. We replayed 2016-2026 week by week, using only what was knowable each day, and published each pattern's actual record in the notes below — including one pattern that failed its own test and is marked accordingly. What the score is: a 0-115 measure of how strongly the stock currently resembles patterns that preceded big gains in the past — built from its sales-growth rank and price-strength rank within all 1,500 stocks, plus the bonuses and penalties listed in each row's pop-up. What it is not: a buy rating — and that is by design, not a flaw. This table works in three layers: the pattern score decides where to look (pure arithmetic, the same formula for all 1,500 stocks, so it can be tested against history and can't quietly play favorites); the tags say what we found when we looked (judgments like "this trade is crowded" that can't be computed from a data feed — shown beside the score, never secretly subtracted from it); and a full investigation decides whether to buy (competition, the reason a stock is cheap, whether the filings' story holds — work no formula can do; the first table on this page already does the arithmetic on cash flow, debt and valuation against the company's own history). Everything measurable is in the math; everything that requires judgment is visible next to it; everything that requires investigation happens before any dollar moves.

Growth speeding up — sales are growing faster than a year ago and the stock is stronger over the past six months than over the past year. This pattern flags stocks mid-run: by design, everything carrying it has already moved. Its record (2016-2025 replay, top-20 flags): 3.2% doubled within a year vs. 1.9% for the average index member; median 12-month return +12.6% vs. +10.4%. A real but modest edge — with a documented catastrophic mode: every top-20 flag from November 2021, the market's most euphoric month, was negative a year later (median about −45%).

Deep low, turning up — current rules (v2): at least 30% below its five-year high, its 52-week low set within the past six months, bounced 15-100% off that low, and a positive three-month return. The original version of this pattern FAILED its replay test: its top-20 flags doubled 5.8% of the time but fell 30%+ a full 21% of the time, with a median outcome of −4.1% while the average index member returned +10.4% — and it missed the decade's best recovery (Nvidia after October 2022) because its safety gate opened late. The v2 rules anchor to a recent low instead; they carry NO historical claim and are being judged on live data from Aug. 11, 2026. Until that verdict, treat these rows with the old warning.

New spin-off — split off from a parent within the past two years, judged on price strength (and sales growth once filings exist — young spin-offs have no year-ago quarter to compare for about a year, so v2 no longer requires it), plus a fixed 10-point bonus for the documented spin-off effect. Its record is the strongest and the least proven: 16.2% of top-20 flags doubled within a year, none halved, median +40% — but that comes from only 11 companies in nine years, dominated by three power-sector spins. Promising, tiny sample.

Scoring changelog — v2 went live Aug. 11, 2026, after the replay: trough-turn redesigned as above; growth flags now lose 10 points if the stock already doubled in the trailing year and 10 more after three straight quarters of accelerating sales (every November 2021 disaster carried both marks); spin-offs qualify before their filings exist; and the universe widened from the S&P 500 to the S&P 1500 — the replay showed the index boundary, not the scoring, is what hid Sandisk in 2025. The records quoted above were measured on v1; v2's record accrues live and will be reported as it comes.

Why Sandisk is No. 1 — and why that is not a buy signal

Ten of Sandisk's points are the fixed spin-off bonus. Even without it, it still leads a tight cluster (104.8 versus Sezzle's 104.1, Corcept's 103.8 and Acadian's 103.4) — its sales growth and six-month strength are at the ceiling of the whole 1,500-stock universe. But note who sits outside the top 20 because of v2's late-cycle penalties: AMD (94.8) and HPE (94.8), both docked 10 points for having already doubled in the trailing year — and, as of today, Datadog (94.0) for the same reason. Sandisk keeps its spot only because spin-offs are exempt from that penalty — a design choice the live forward test will judge.

Two more cautions. Sandisk has already gained about 162% in six months: the pattern was flagged mid-run, not early. And our own research (see "Scanned and passed" below) calls memory chips the most crowded trade in the market right now. A stock can match a winning pattern and still be a poor buy at today's price. This page shows both facts rather than hiding either.

The replay added a finding: no version of this page could have flagged Sandisk in August 2025, because Sandisk did not join the S&P 500 until late 2025 — and its filings had no year-ago quarter to compute growth from until mid-2026. The index boundary, not the scoring, was the constraint. Expanding coverage beyond the S&P 500 is the roadmap answer.

What this list cannot see

Every pattern here requires movement that has already begun — sales accelerating, a stock climbing off a low. The list is blind to a coiled spring: a company whose order book is surging while its stock goes nowhere. Huntington Ingalls, Northrop Grumman, Constellation Energy and EMCOR — the names our industry research currently likes most — are exactly that shape, which is why they appear in the theme sleeves below and not in this table.

If they start moving, the screen will catch them. The sleeves are how we try to be early instead of waiting for that.

Theme sleeves — the bottleneck lens · demand rising × supply held back by something slow to fix; ranked by how durable the bottleneck is and how much is not yet priced in (research dated Aug. 11)

1 · Defense choke points, including shipbuilding

Demand is locked in by signed contracts, not headlines: a roughly $1 trillion U.S. defense budget, NATO members pledging 5% of GDP by 2035, and restocking after the Iran war (an interceptor-award figure this page used to carry was removed Aug. 16 — a primary-source check could not find it in any Lockheed filing; the award actually booked in the June quarter looks like a roughly $35 billion undefinitized THAAD contract, and submarine awards in July came to $76.6 billion). Supply is gated by things that take years to fix — rocket-motor plants (two scaled makers), shipyard labor (submarines built at about 1.2 a year against a need of 2 until about 2032) and interceptor production lines. Mostly independent of AI spending.

HII 17.0× earnings · $57B of signed orders · −28% off high NOC 19.8× · record $105B order book · flat 52 wk LMT 19.5× · $230B · new orders 3.2× sales RNMBY 26× · −30%/12 mo on record orders

What would kill it: a durable Ukraine ceasefire (Europe first) or the $350 billion piece of the fiscal 2027 budget failing. Crowded corners to avoid: RTX at 30× earnings, drone stocks at 60–68×.

2 · Nuclear fuel cycle and baseload power

Uranium term prices are at an 18-year high. The U.S. can enrich only about 29% of the fuel it uses, with Russian supply banned through 2040. Big cloud companies have signed 20-year contracts for about 10 gigawatts of nuclear power. The longest-lasting bottleneck in our scan — and the 2025 retail frenzy has already collapsed (OKLO −73%), which is when entry prices get interesting.

CEG 22.9× · −33% off high · restart ruling due May 2027 LEU 78× · only U.S. enricher · orders booked to 2040 BWXT · CCJ

What would kill it: the restart license denied, waivers to the Russian-fuel ban or small-reactor delays compounding.

3 · Power equipment and grid labor

The U.S. grid authority raised its 10-year peak-demand forecast by 224 gigawatts. Gas-turbine delivery slots are selling for 2031. Transformers take about 128 weeks to get, at prices up 77% since 2019, and data-center construction is short roughly 450,000 electricians, a trade with a four- to five-year apprenticeship. The famous names are fully priced; the second tier is not.

EME 23.5× · record $17.1B of signed future work · profit +35% POWL · biggest order book in its 79-year history · −36% off high MOD 24× · $4B prepaid cooling deal Already priced: GEV 40×+ · PWR 34× · VRT · ETN

What would kill it — and the sleeve's shared kill switch: one big cloud company cutting its spending forecast. Demand here lives on quarterly budgets, unlike themes 1 and 2.

4 · Derated franchises — quality on sale

Not a bottleneck theme — a mispricing theme, and the site's founding mandate. Four franchises whose 2023-25 deratings (weight-loss-drug fear, rate fear) overshot while 2026 results show demand intact: Insulet at 21× earnings against its own history of 49-227×, down 53%, while growing revenue 23.5%. Pool Corp. at 18× versus its usual 30-35×, with new-pool construction already at 2019 trough levels — the bad news is in. Danaher at 24×, the only laggard in a lab-tools recovery that already lifted its peers 30-47%. Dexcom at the bottom of its own range with guidance raised.

PODD 20.7× · record-low multiple · −53% off high POOL 17.7× · vs. 30-35× own history DHR 23.7× · peers already re-rated DXCM 31× · vs. 40-169× own band

What would kill it, per name: Insulet's type-2 stumble proving demand-side; pool permits still falling into 2027 with no rate relief (markets price no 2026 cuts and real odds of a September hike); Danaher's bioprocessing orders rolling back under +10%.

5 · U.S. natural gas and LNG

Producers are priced off a weak $2.77 spot market with stocks near 52-week lows, while the demand pull is structural and dated: a wave of gas-export terminals starts up 2026-28, and data centers are signing for gas power. The bottleneck is export capacity itself — multi-year, permitted, capital-intensive — and European gas still costs roughly four times the U.S. price. The one theme every earlier scan missed.

EQT 15.8× · near 52-wk low EXE 11.8× · −22% off high LNG (Cheniere) 15.4× · the tollbooth

What would kill it: record supply keeps outrunning the export pull and 2027 prices stay under about $3.50 — then the cheap stay cheap. Avoid pipeline stocks as the expression (WMB at 31× near its high).

6 · Specialty insurance underwriters

Insurance pricing is softening — the steepest property-catastrophe rate declines since the late 1990s — and the street has already given up on the two franchise names: Kinsale at an all-time-low 18× earnings and 4.1× book (versus its own 4.6-8.1× history) while earning a 24% return on equity; Progressive near the bottom of its own range and a direct beneficiary of cheaper reinsurance. The bet is that the capitulation overshot, not that the cycle turns back up.

KNSL 17.8× · target below price PGR 13.3× · bottom of own band

The honest crux: 2026 profits are flattered by zero Atlantic hurricanes so far — if soft pricing runs to 2028 or reserves deteriorate, today's "cheap" sits on peak earnings. RNR near its high is the trap in this group.

7 · Pharma patent cliff — residual only

Big pharma loses exclusivity on its biggest drugs in 2026-28 (Keytruda alone is about 47% of Merck's revenue) and is buying replacements at escalating premiums — but biotech indexes are up 86% in a year, so the obvious trade is spent. What's left: platform companies the acquirers still need that just got re-derated, led by Alnylam, down 56% from its high after an Aug. 11 guidance cut.

ALNY · −56% off high · fresh knife, let it settle INSM · −38% IONS · −35%

What would kill it: the deal wave breaking (rates, regulators), or Alnylam's problem proving competitive rather than one-off. Venture-style sizing only.

8 · Obesity and metabolic medicine

The market leader is guiding to about $87 billion in revenue, up 48%. Pill versions are underperforming, so injectable drugs — and the sterile filling lines they require — remain the constraint. Those lines take three to five years to certify, and the more than $25 billion Lilly and Novo are spending to build their own proves how scarce outside capacity is. The pure-play contract manufacturers are private.

LLY · earnings beat +27%, still climbing WST #46 · re-rated to 38× TMO #140 NVO · the cheap No. 2, contested

What would kill it: a drug-safety surprise, prices falling faster than volume grows, or the drugmakers' own factories coming online in 2027-28.

9 · Mental health and neuroscience

Diagnosis and treatment rates keep rising, and new drug mechanisms — including psychedelic therapies — are in late-stage trials. Mostly small, all-or-nothing companies: venture-style position sizes or nothing.

ABBV #119 · +23% JNJ · not yet ranked CMPS · pre-revenue

What would kill it: trial failures or regulators reversing course.

How the calls have fared

These are the engine's own calls, scored forward and never restated; nothing here feeds back into its rules.

Method. Deep-low scores: 0.6 × how far below the five-year high (percentile) + 0.25 × rebound size (percentile) + 15 if filings confirm the turn (sales accelerating or margins improving); the stock must be above a rising 200-day average, so falling knives are excluded by construction. Growth-inflection scores: 0.5 × growth percentile + 0.45 × six-month-return percentile + 10 if sales are accelerating. Spin-offs add a fixed 10. Fundamentals come from SEC filings, prices are daily closes and news tone is the average sentiment of the last roughly 50 articles per name (Alpha Vantage), scored on the top rows only.

Honesty box. No expected returns appear here because none can be computed honestly. The pattern records above come from a week-by-week replay of 2016-2026 using only information knowable on each date — index membership as of the date, filings by their filing date, prices through that Friday. Four caveats travel with those numbers. The model that designed these patterns was trained on this same history, so the replay can kill bad patterns but cannot prove good ones; a live forward test started Aug. 11, 2026, with immutable weekly snapshots. About 18% of companies that left the index have no retrievable price history, so the numbers are flattered by the survivors. The counts are stock-weeks, not independent bets — one stock flagged for 40 straight weeks is one episode. And in the six months after the model's knowledge ends, every pattern has so far trailed the average index member. Known data issues, flagged in the rows themselves: Sandisk's +251% and SM Energy's +215% sales-growth figures look like acquisition or spin-off accounting rather than organic growth and are under review; Chord Energy's +84% was checked and is real, but roughly 90% of it is a jump in realized crude prices, not more barrels. Waters is still in the table, at 94.9, and its +91% sales growth is confirmed merger accounting — the BD Biosciences deal, not underlying demand — so the row carries a Data check tag. Micron's +346% is still under review but matches no pattern today, so it does not appear. Six rows carry a Crowded trade tag tonight, down from seven: American Water stopped matching any pattern and left the list.
