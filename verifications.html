All 24 values the model relied on match the filed statements. The model still marks Datadog's evidence as conflicting, because one of its own checks misreads how the company computes diluted earnings per share.

What the matching numbers do not show

  • The model's run records a failed check on fiscal 2025 diluted earnings per share. The check divides net income of $107.7 million by diluted shares and gets $0.30, against the filed $0.31. Datadog has convertible notes, and the filed calculation adds $3.7 million of note interest after tax back to the numerator, which gives $0.31. The filing is right and the check is wrong.
  • Stock compensation of $823.0 million is 67% of operating cash flow and 20.7% of revenue for the year to June 30, 2026.

  • Operating income for the year to June 30, 2026, is $16.3 million, a margin of 0.4%. Other income of $183.5 million, most of it interest on cash, exceeds net income of $177.6 million.
  • Accounts payable added $106.1 million to operating cash flow in the latest period. The latest quarterly report ties the rise to the timing of cloud hosting invoices.
  • The latest quarterly report says the largest customer reduced its usage from the third quarter of 2026, which may slow revenue growth.

How a check works

The first check reads the income statement, cash flow statement and balance sheet in the filed documents. A trailing twelve-month figure is the latest fiscal year plus the current year to date minus the prior year to date.

A value matches when it is within 0.5% of the filing or within the filing's rounding. Each company is checked on the same 24 values, and any further value is shown but not counted.

Filings report no fourth-quarter share average, so the twelve-month diluted share rows repeat the model's own construction from filed inputs. They are a reproduction and not an independent test.

The second reviewer is a different model from a different company. It receives the first check's rows and claims, reads the filings itself, and is told to look for mistakes.

The first two reviews downloaded the filings a second time. Later reviews read the copies of the SEC documents that the first check downloaded, so both rule on the same documents and nothing is downloaded twice. That reviewer has no network access and cannot open a document the first check did not save; each review lists what it could not check.

Record. The same checks are published as machine-readable JSON, with the components of every calculation and the filing each came from. These checks use no personal data.
